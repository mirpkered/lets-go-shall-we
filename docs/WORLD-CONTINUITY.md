# Connective Tissue / World Continuity Appendix

Apply this appendix to newly authored adventures unless a scenario-specific instruction conflicts with it. The central aim is a library of self-contained stories that occasionally rewards memory and feels like one shared world over a long-lived character’s journey. There is no mandatory overarching plot, campaign order, or requirement to reference earlier stories.

## Fresh-character and character-history rules

- Every adventure must be understandable and completable by a fresh character. Previous history may add context, alter dialogue or trust, reveal a small alternate approach, or deepen a moment, but never supplies the premise, danger, objective, or basic solution.
- Distinguish the player’s memory from the current character’s knowledge. Use only history and knowledge carried by that living character; do not leak a dead, abandoned, or retired character’s personal knowledge into a new traveler.
- Use specific remembered actions, not generic fame. There is no fame, karma, morality, heroism, alignment, or faction score. Any narrow recognition should be grounded in a relevant deed and a plausible path by which the NPC learned of it.
- History belongs to the character. World consequences may outlast death when people, places, property, or public events could realistically preserve or spread them. Do not recognize a new traveler as a dead or retired one; their deeds may still be remembered.
- Keep history callbacks concise and selective. Prefer one relevant reaction over a recap, and give characters without the flag a natural first-meeting version. A callback should reward memory, deepen continuity, affect trust or information, enable an alternate interaction, or acknowledge a consequence; otherwise omit it.

## People, places, and events

- Before naming an NPC, inspect the current roster and prefer unused names. Names such as Eli and Mara are already common; avoid casual reuse. Reuse an existing person only when the recurrence is intentional and explicit. Geography, profession, elapsed time, and prior circumstances must fit. A repeated name alone does not establish identity.
- When a person returns, make their response reflect the actual prior encounter where practical: recognition, gratitude, wariness, awkwardness, or simple familiarity. Fresh characters receive a normal introduction.
- Consider established towns, roads, inns, mines, farms, waterways, ferries, rail lines, and settlements when a return or brief reference fits. Preserve known geography and facts; do not force a cameo or place someone implausibly far away without explanation.
- Significant public events and lasting outcomes may echo later through proportionate word of mouth, notices, newspapers, letters, merchants, innkeepers, rail workers, or other period-appropriate channels. Private minor incidents do not become regional legend. Avoid impossible timing, especially for injured or displaced people.
- Keep persistent consequences concrete and selective: a repaired crossing, closed road, damaged inn, surviving worker, lost property, recovering family, abandoned structure, or relocated business can matter later when a plausible witness or route connects the stories. Repeated rail lines, road names, foods, sayings, ferry routes, and trade paths can make the world familiar without adding lore for its own sake.
- A recurring employer or contact may create an earned opportunity through a specific referral. Use this sparingly; not every job comes from prior deeds, and recognition stays local rather than universal.
- A recurring supernatural person, image, sound, or phenomenon should preserve its known characteristics, recur rarely, and remain unexplained where appropriate. Do not turn repetition into constant exposition.

## Items, knowledge, and provenance

- Preserve the origin of distinctive property and unusual rewards. Where practical, record who owned it or how it was acquired, then let a later recognition invite explanation, a request, suspicion, or gratitude. Do not automatically punish the player or create a theft score. Ordinary rope and gloves are not famous artifacts.
- When a character knowingly keeps recognizable property found unattended, use descriptive character-history data for the specific object and owner; the actual item still follows the normal carry-over rules. Do not treat bank contents, human-player memory, or another character’s inventory as current-character knowledge.
- Audit item use in both directions: new rewards against existing adventures, and existing carryable items against a new adventure. Prefer strong natural uses; do not add interactions merely to raise item counts. Keep each item’s identity distinct and do not let one tool quietly solve half the library.
- Before finalizing a reward, ask whether it overlaps an existing item, an existing reward could serve instead, the new item creates worthwhile future hooks, it has a strong backward retrofit, or no material reward better serves the story. Avoid reward inflation.
- Preserve the Tool Capability Rule and Equipment Accessibility Rule. A callback never improves an item’s physical abilities. Only offer gear that the fiction puts within reach and in usable condition.
- Keep supernatural items narrow and atmospheric. Review established artifacts before adding another; an item may react, reveal, warn, or alter one specific interaction, but is not a universal detector, key, or combat upgrade.
- Character knowledge can unlock recognition of a particular symbol, route, custom, or artifact only when that character actually learned it. Do not grant facts because the human player has seen another adventure.

## Rare Easter eggs

- Easter eggs are occasional flavor, never gameplay: they cannot be required to understand or finish an adventure, change odds or endings, grant items or money, alter history/knowledge, or affect carry or traveler progression.
- Use only a small, context-appropriate eligible-scene pool and a low chance (about 1–3% per eligible scene entry); allow no more than one event per run. Most runs should have none.
- Keep references legible as ordinary odd remarks or coincidences to players who miss them. They do not establish crossover canon, require follow-up, or use long recognizable quotations.
- Preserve the late-19th-century-inspired setting and avoid modern technology. Do not place a joke where it would undercut danger, grief, serious injury, rescue, or an emotionally weighty ending.
- Persist the selected event with the active run so reload cannot reroll it. Favor unseen events using a small local recent-seen list; do not expose a collection checklist or reward discovery.

## Authoring workflow and guardrails

Before implementation, inspect the scenario roster, continuity registry, relevant character-history flags, item catalog/provenance, established locations, and major events. Note strong natural connections; do not invent one to satisfy a quota. Keep callbacks conditional and give every one a fresh-character fallback.

After implementation, record only the strongest new reusable candidates: a memorable NPC, a distinctive place or item, a notable public event, or an unresolved supernatural detail. Update the lightweight registry, not a sprawling lore database. State which opportunities were deliberately skipped when reporting a batch if that helps explain the design.

Where callbacks are implemented, test fresh-character fallback, the relevant-history variant and its no-history counterpart, item/NPC recognition, save persistence, absence of dead-character knowledge leaks, and independence from play order. Keep stories fully playable when no previous adventure has been seen.

For a scenario or batch report, summarize callbacks and recurring people/places actually used, prior-history or item-recognition reactions, any new reusable elements, and the strongest opportunities deliberately skipped. A low callback count is not a defect.

If a callback does not fit naturally, let the adventure stand alone. Occasional meaningful recognition is more valuable than constant references.
